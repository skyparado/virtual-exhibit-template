THE DISK:

Magnetic Storage and HDDs

by Yamsuan, Rhian Claire V. · ~12 minute read

Introduction

Before the solid-state drive, before the USB flash drive, and long before the cloud, data lived on spinning disks. From IBM's refrigerator-sized RAMAC 350 in 1956 — which stored a mere 5 megabytes across fifty 24-inch platters — to the 20-terabyte drives sold today, the hard disk drive (HDD) defined the landscape of secondary storage for more than six decades.

This section traces that evolution: how magnetic recording principles were harnessed to encode binary data, how the mechanical architecture of platters, read/write heads, and actuator arms was refined across generations, and how the HDD's unique random-access capability reshaped the memory hierarchy of every computer system built between the 1950s and the 2010s.

Magnetic Recording Principles

At the core of every hard drive is a deceptively simple physical fact: magnetic materials can be polarized in one of two stable orientations, and that orientation can be detected and changed by a nearby magnetic field. This binary stability — north or south, 1 or 0 — is what makes magnetic media a natural fit for storing digital information (Seagate, 2023).

Writing Data

A write head consists of a tiny electromagnet with a gap cut into its core. When current flows through the coil, it generates a fringing magnetic field at that gap — strong enough to polarize the magnetic grains on the disk surface directly beneath it. Reversing the current reverses the field direction, writing the opposite bit. Because the disk is spinning rapidly beneath the head, each polarity reversal is laid down as a short magnetized region along a circular track (Patterson & Hennessy, 2017).

Reading Data

Early drives used an inductive read head — the same coil that wrote data could also detect the voltage induced as magnetized regions passed beneath it. Modern drives use a separate magnetoresistive (MR) read element, whose electrical resistance changes in response to the magnetic field of the passing data regions. This approach is far more sensitive, allowing the detection of much smaller and more densely packed magnetic domains (Iwasaki, 1980).

Perpendicular Magnetic Recording (PMR)

For most of the HDD's history, bits were recorded longitudinally — the magnetic domains lay flat along the surface, end to end. As areal density pushed higher, neighboring domains began interfering with each other, creating a physical limit. The shift to perpendicular magnetic recording, which orients domains vertically (standing on end, like fence posts), dramatically reduced this interference and enabled far greater bit density on the same physical area. Seagate and Hitachi introduced commercial PMR drives in 2005 (Kryder, 2005).

Platters, Read/Write Heads, and Actuator Arms

A modern hard disk drive is a precision mechanical assembly. Understanding its components reveals why it performs as it does — and why each part became a target for engineering improvement across decades.

Platters

Platters are rigid disks, typically made of aluminum or glass, coated with a thin layer of ferromagnetic material — originally iron oxide, later cobalt-based alloys for higher coercivity. A single drive may contain one to eight stacked platters, each with two recordable surfaces, spinning at a constant speed. Consumer drives typically spin at 5,400 or 7,200 RPM; high-performance enterprise drives run at 10,000 or even 15,000 RPM (Seagate, 2023).

Data is organized on each surface in concentric rings called tracks, which are further subdivided into sectors (traditionally 512 bytes, more recently 4,096 bytes with Advanced Format). The set of tracks at the same radial position across all platter surfaces forms a cylinder.

Read/Write Heads

Each platter surface has a dedicated read/write head. These heads do not physically touch the surface — they float on a thin film of air (the "air bearing") generated by the spinning platter, at a distance of just a few nanometers. This flying height has shrunk dramatically over decades: early IBM drives flew at around 20 micrometers; modern drives fly at under 5 nanometers, a distance smaller than most virus particles (Patterson & Hennessy, 2017).

Actuator Arm

All the heads are mounted on a single actuator arm assembly that pivots on a voice-coil actuator — an electromagnet that moves the arm across the platter radius with high precision. When the drive needs to access a different track, the actuator swings the heads to the correct radial position. This movement is called a seek, and the time it takes — seek time — is one of the primary performance bottlenecks of an HDD. A typical modern consumer drive has an average seek time of 8–12 milliseconds (Western Digital, 2022).

Random Access vs. Sequential Access

One of the HDD's defining advantages over its predecessor — magnetic tape — was its ability to access data in any order without rewinding through intervening content. This capability is called random access, and it transformed how operating systems and databases could be designed.

Sequential Access

In sequential access, data is read or written in a fixed linear order — each byte follows the previous one along the track. Magnetic tape operates this way: to reach data at position 1,000, you must fast-forward past positions 1 through 999. Sequential access is efficient when reading large contiguous files (streaming video, large backups) but impractical for workloads that require jumping between unrelated locations (Tanenbaum, 2014).

Random Access

An HDD can swing its actuator arm to any track within milliseconds, then wait for the platter to rotate the target sector beneath the head — a delay called rotational latency. On a 7,200 RPM drive, average rotational latency is about 4.2 milliseconds. The combination of seek time and rotational latency gives an effective random-access latency of roughly 10–20 milliseconds — far slower than DRAM (nanoseconds), but orders of magnitude faster than tape (seconds to minutes).

This random-access capability made the HDD the natural home for file systems, relational databases, and virtual memory swap space — workloads that constantly jump between unrelated data locations (Patterson & Hennessy, 2017).

Storage Capacity Scaling and Areal Density

Areal density measures how many bits can be stored per unit area of disk surface, typically expressed in gigabits per square inch (Gb/in²). It is the fundamental driver of HDD capacity growth — the same physical disk form factor stores more data with each generation simply by packing bits closer together.

From 1956 to the early 2000s, areal density roughly doubled every 18 months — a pace that tracked closely with Moore's Law in semiconductors. This compounding improvement of approximately 60–100% per year was the engine behind HDD capacity growing from 5 MB in 1956 to multiple terabytes by the 2010s (Kryder, 2005).

Technologies That Enabled Growth

Several engineering breakthroughs drove successive waves of areal density improvement: the introduction of thin-film inductive heads in the 1980s; the adoption of magnetoresistive (MR) read heads in the 1990s; giant magnetoresistance (GMR) heads in the late 1990s, recognized with the 2007 Nobel Prize in Physics; and perpendicular magnetic recording (PMR) in 2005. More recent techniques include shingled magnetic recording (SMR) and heat-assisted magnetic recording (HAMR), still in commercial deployment today (Western Digital, 2022).

HDD Role in the Memory Hierarchy

Computer architects organize storage into a hierarchy based on speed, cost, and capacity. Faster storage is more expensive per byte and therefore smaller; slower storage is cheaper and therefore larger. The HDD occupied the secondary storage tier of this hierarchy for decades — below RAM in speed and cost, but above magnetic tape in both (Patterson & Hennessy, 2017).

Because the HDD is orders of magnitude slower than DRAM but orders of magnitude cheaper per byte, operating systems use it for virtual memory — swapping pages of RAM content to disk when physical memory is full. File systems, databases, and application data all reside permanently on the HDD, loaded into RAM only when needed. This two-tier arrangement of DRAM + HDD was the dominant computing model from the 1970s until SSDs became cost-competitive in the early 2010s (Tanenbaum, 2014).
